# NASTY! Monetization Research

Prepared 26 July 2026. All prices are US App Store, checked on the dates below.

---

## PART 1: The short version, for Blake

**Recommendation: launch it free, put no ads in it ever, and add one optional "Supporter" purchase at $4.99 that unlocks cosmetic extras only. Enrol in Apple's Small Business Program on day one so Apple takes 15% instead of 30%. Do not build a subscription.**

Here is the honest reason, and you are not going to love it.

I looked up the two closest things to NASTY! that actually exist on the App Store. One is *Robicheau Wahoo*, which is literally another guy's family board game that he digitised. It is free, has no ads, and has **3 ratings**. The other is *iAggravation*, a paid $1.99 marble game that has been on the store since 2012 and has **20 ratings**. Neither of these made anybody any money. That is the realistic ceiling for a family board game with no marketing budget, and it is the single most important fact in this document. The pricing decision is not going to change your income in any meaningful way, so you should make it on other grounds.

**On your ads question specifically, you have one fact wrong and it matters.** Apple does not have an ad network that puts ads inside your game. They shut that down in 2016. "Apple Search Ads" is the opposite thing: it is you *paying Apple* to advertise NASTY! on the App Store. To actually put ads in your game you would sign up with Google AdMob, install their code, and let Google fill the ad slots.

And here is what that would pay you. With your current audience, which is your extended family, so call it 30 to 50 people, ads would earn you somewhere around **$2 to $5 a month**. AdMob does not even send you a cheque until you have accumulated $100, so you would get paid roughly once every two years. If NASTY! somehow grew to 5,000 monthly players, which would be a genuine hit for a game like this, you would be looking at roughly **$150 to $600 a month**. Real money, but you are nowhere near that and probably never will be.

**And there is a trap in the ads idea you have not hit yet.** Your game is rated 4+ and kids actually play it. Apple's rules (guideline 2.5.18) say ads in a 4+ game cannot use behavioural targeting on kids and must be age-appropriate, and US law (COPPA, which got tightened this year) means you would need parental consent before any ad network could track a child user for targeting. The practical effect is you would be limited to plain contextual ads, which pay the *least*. So the version of ads you are legally allowed to run in a kids' game is the version that earns the least money. You would be adding legal exposure, a Google SDK, a privacy policy obligation, and pop-ups in your family's game, in exchange for the price of a coffee each month.

**So why free-with-a-tip-jar rather than just charging $2.99?**

Three reasons specific to your game:

1. **Your game has online multiplayer.** A paywall on an unknown multiplayer game gets you an empty lobby. Multiplayer only works if there are people in it. Every dollar of friction at the door costs you the thing that makes the game good.
2. **You would be charging your own relatives.** They are your entire user base right now. Billing your cousins $2.99 for the family game is a weird conversation, and the free-vs-paid revenue difference here is maybe $60 a year.
3. **Free apps are 95% of the App Store** for a reason ([Statista, Feb 2026: 95.24% of iOS apps are free](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1020996/distribution-of-free-and-paid-ios-apps/)). An unknown paid app from an unknown developer with no reviews and no marketing gets close to zero organic downloads.

**If you would rather just recoup your costs and be done with it,** the fallback is: charge **$2.99 flat**, no ads, no IAP. At 15% Apple commission you keep $2.54 per sale, so **39 sales pays your entire $99 Apple fee for the year**. That is completely achievable from family and friends alone. Apple gives you 100 free promo codes per version, so you can hand the game to your relatives for free anyway. This is a perfectly respectable choice. It just closes the door on the game ever spreading.

**What I would actually do:** free, Supporter IAP at $4.99, Small Business Program. Watch it for a year. If it ever passes about 10,000 downloads, come back and we will revisit, because at that scale the maths genuinely changes. Below that, treat NASTY! as a thing you made for your family that happens to be on the App Store, and let it cost you the $99. That is not a failure. It is what it is.

**One sequencing warning:** you can change the price whenever you like, but anyone who downloads it while it is free keeps it free forever. So free-then-paid works, but you never get to bill the early crowd. Paid-then-free annoys the people who paid. Free is the more reversible choice.

## PART 3: The prevailing models, and which one fits a small indie

| Model | Who uses it | Realistic revenue for a few hundred to a few thousand users |
|---|---|---|
| **Paid upfront** | 5.2% of all App Store apps ([app-store stats, 2026](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/app-store-statistics/)); brand titles and deliberate "premium, no ads" indies | $2.54 net per download at $2.99 with Small Business rate. 500 downloads = ~$1,270 lifetime. But downloads collapse: see Part 6. |
| **Free + ads + one-time remove-ads IAP** | The commonest indie pattern (Ludo Trouble $2.99) | Ads: pennies (Part 4). Remove-ads conversion in casual games is typically 1% to 3%, so 1,000 users buys you ~20 unlocks = ~$50 net. |
| **Free + ads + subscription** | Almost nobody in board games. Subscriptions in this category are Season Passes on live-ops games (Parchisi STAR $4.99, Sorry! World $9.99), not ad-removal subs | Requires constant new content to avoid churn and refund complaints. Bad fit. |
| **Freemium with cosmetics / soft currency** | UNO, Yahtzee, Ludo King, Parchisi STAR, Catan | Needs tens of thousands of DAU and a live-ops team to work at all. |
| **Genuinely free, no monetisation** | Robicheau Wahoo, and most personal-project board games | $0. |

**Most common for small indie board games specifically: free with ads plus a single flat remove-ads purchase around $2.99.** That is the observed pattern. It is also, for Blake, the wrong one, for the kids reasons in Part 5.

**Realistic revenue at his scale.** For an app with a few hundred users there is no monetisation model that produces meaningful income. At a few thousand engaged users the numbers become real but modest: roughly $150 to $600 a month from ads, or a few hundred dollars total from a remove-ads IAP. The honest framing is that monetisation matters below about 10,000 users only as a way to offset hosting costs.

## PART 4: Correcting the ad question, with numbers

### 4.1 Apple has no in-app ad network. This is the core correction.

Apple ran an in-app ad network called **iAd** from 2010. Apple announced in January 2016 that it would shut down, and terminated all Developer Advertising Services Agreements on **30 June 2016 at 11:59am Pacific** ([AppleInsider, 18 May 2016](https://appleinsider.com/articles/16/05/18/apple-reminds-developers-of-impending-iad-app-network-shutdown-apis-to-be-deprecated); [Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/apple-is-shuttering-iad-app-network-at-the-end-of-june/412284/)). It has never been replaced.

What Apple sells today is **Apple Ads** (formerly Apple Search Ads), at [ads.apple.com/app-store](https://ads.apple.com/app-store/). Apple's own description: "Ads appear in a variety of carefully integrated placements throughout the App Store." The four placements are the Today tab, the Search tab, Search results, and the bottom of Product pages. **This is a tool for Blake to pay Apple to promote NASTY! to App Store shoppers.** It is money out, not money in. It is the exact opposite of what he asked about.

### 4.2 What putting ads in NASTY! would actually involve

- Sign up for **Google AdMob** (or AppLovin, Unity Ads, ironSource). AdMob and AppLovin are the two dominant networks: in Q1 2026 AppLovin held 39% of iOS ad revenue and AdMob led Android at 24% ([Tenjin Ad Monetization Benchmark Report 2026](https://tenjin.com/blog/ad-mon-gaming-2026/), updated 23 April 2026, based on 146 billion impressions).
- Integrate their SDK into the app. This is a real code change, so a new binary and a new App Review submission.
- Add a privacy policy, complete Apple's privacy nutrition label for the ad SDK's data collection, and declare in-app advertising in the age rating questionnaire (see 5.4).
- Handle consent (ATT prompt on iOS, plus child-directed tagging, see Part 5).

**Revenue share.** Third-party sources consistently put the AdMob network split at roughly **60% to the publisher, 40% to Google** ([MonetizePros](https://monetizepros.com/ad-network-reviews/admob/)). *Not verified against a Google-published figure.* Google does not publish an official AdMob network split. The eCPM numbers AdMob reports in its dashboard are already publisher-side (net), so do not deduct 40% twice.

**Payout threshold.** AdMob's minimum payout is widely reported as **$100**, Net-21. *Sourced from third-party reporting ([MonetizePros](https://monetizepros.com/ad-network-reviews/admob/)), not verified against Google's own help page.* This matters enormously at Blake's scale.

**One genuine upside worth stating:** Apple takes **no commission on advertising revenue**. Ad money is not an in-app purchase, so the 15%/30% cut does not apply.

### 4.4 The actual monthly number for NASTY!

Assumptions, stated so they can be argued with:
- DAU/MAU ratio of **20%**. This is generous for a casual board game; 10% to 15% is more typical.
- **3 ad impressions per active user per day** (one interstitial between games, plus a banner). More than that in a family game would be obnoxious.
- eCPM range of **$2 (kid-safe contextual only, see Part 5) to $5 (interstitial, mixed audience)**.

| Scenario | MAU | DAU | Impressions/month | At $2 eCPM | At $5 eCPM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Blake today** (extended family) | 40 | 8 | 720 | **$1.44/mo** | **$3.60/mo** |
| Small success | 500 | 100 | 9,000 | **$18/mo** | **$45/mo** |
| Genuine hit for this genre | 5,000 | 1,000 | 90,000 | **$180/mo** | **$450/mo** |

**Cross-check against ARPDAU benchmarks.** Casual and puzzle games run **$0.03 to $0.10 ARPDAU**; hyper-casual runs **$0.01 to $0.05** ([Juego Studio ARPDAU benchmarks, 2024-2025 data from Appodeal / AppsFlyer / Sensor Tower](https://www.juegostudio.com/blog/arpdau-benchmarks-by-game-genre)). At 1,000 DAU and $0.02 to $0.05 ARPDAU that is $600 to $1,500 a month, but those benchmarks are for heavily optimised games with aggressive ad loads, rewarded video, and hybrid IAP. My $180 to $450 estimate for a light, family-friendly, contextual-only ad load is the appropriate figure.

**Independent cross-check.** A worked AdMob example with 1,000 DAU, $5 eCPM and 3 ads per day per user produces $15/day, about **$450/month** ([Bofu AdMob calculator writeup](https://bofu.ca/en/blogs/calculateurs/calculateur-de-revenus-google-admob)). A developer with 10,000 active Android users reported **$400 to $900/month** ([Quora, developer self-report, anecdotal](https://www.quora.com/How-much-I-can-make-with-admob-with-10000-app-downloads)). My model lines up.

**The killer detail: at Blake's real audience size, ads pay roughly $2 to $4 a month, and AdMob will not send money until $100 has accumulated. That is a payout every two to four years.** He would install a Google tracking SDK into a children's game, take on COPPA obligations, and put pop-ups in front of his nieces and nephews, for a cheque that arrives less often than the Winter Olympics.

## PART 5: The kids problem

This is the part Blake has not thought about, and it independently disqualifies the ad plan.

### 5.1 4+ rating is not the same as the Kids Category

Important distinction. **A 4+ age rating does not put an app in the Kids Category.** The Kids Category is a separate opt-in in App Store Connect ([Apple, Kids Apps](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/kids-apps/)). Blake is at 4+ but presumably not in the Kids Category, so the strictest rules do not currently bind him. But 4+ still carries real obligations, and if he ever opted into the Kids Category for discoverability, ads become effectively impossible.

### 5.2 If he ever entered the Kids Category, ads are essentially off the table

Guideline 1.3, quoted verbatim from [Apple's App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/):

> "Apps in the Kids Category should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising. This provides a safer experience for kids. In limited cases, third-party analytics may be permitted provided that the services do not collect or transmit the IDFA or any identifiable information about children (such as name, date of birth, email address), their location, or their devices... Third-party contextual advertising may also be permitted in limited cases provided that the services have publicly documented practices and policies for Kids Category apps that include human review of ad creatives for age appropriateness."

Also: "Kids Category apps may not send personally identifiable information or device information to third parties." An AdMob SDK does exactly that by default.

Note that guideline 1.3 has a one-way ratchet: "once customers expect your app to follow the Kids Category requirements, it will need to continue to meet these guidelines in subsequent updates, **even if you decide to deselect the category**."

### 5.3 Even outside the Kids Category, guideline 2.5.18 binds him

Verbatim:

> "Ads displayed in an app must be appropriate for the app's age rating, allow the user to see all information used to target them for that ad (without requiring the user to leave the app), and **may not engage in targeted or behavioral advertising based on sensitive user data such as... kids** (e.g. from apps in the App Store's Kids Category), etc. Interstitial ads or ads that interrupt or block the user experience must clearly indicate that they are an ad, must not manipulate or trick users into tapping into them, and must provide easily accessible and visible close/skip buttons large enough for people to easily dismiss the ad. **Apps that contain ads must also include the ability for users to report any inappropriate or age-inappropriate ads.**"

So even at plain 4+, Blake would have to: only serve ads rated appropriate for 4+, provide a visible ad-reporting mechanism inside the game, and use generous close buttons. That is real engineering work for $3 a month.

### 5.4 Apple's 2025 age rating overhaul makes him declare ads

Apple rebuilt the age rating questionnaire in July 2025, adding 13+, 16+ and 18+ to the existing 4+ and 9+ ([Apple Developer News](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=ks775ehf); [9to5Mac, 24 July 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/07/24/apple-notifies-developers-of-new-app-store-age-rating-system/)). The new "Capabilities" section explicitly requires declaring **in-app advertising**, and the answer feeds the generated rating. Deadline to complete the updated questionnaire was **31 January 2026**, so this is already live and Blake should confirm he has answered it for NASTY!. Adding ads would mean re-answering it and could push the rating above 4+.

### 5.5 COPPA, which got stricter this year

The FTC finalised amendments to the Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule in January 2025 ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data); [Federal Register, 22 April 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)). **Full compliance has been required since 22 April 2026** ([Finnegan analysis](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/coppas-amended-rule-is-now-in-full-effect-what-operators-need-to-know.html); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/coppa-rule-amendment-compliance-deadline-approaches)).

The two points that matter for Blake:

- **Behavioural / targeted advertising now requires separate, standalone verifiable parental consent** before disclosing a child's online activity to third parties for ad targeting. It can no longer be bundled into a general consent ([Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/01/alerts-practices-dpc-ftc-issues-long-awaited-new-coppa-rules); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/unpacking-ftcs-coppa-amendments-what-you-need-know)).
- **Contextual advertising remains permitted** without separate parental consent, because the FTC kept the "support for internal operations" exception intact for contextual ads ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/coppas-coming-of-age-key-compliance-changes-in-ftcs-final-rule)).

COPPA applies to services "directed to children" and to any operator with **actual knowledge** of child users. A family board game his relatives' kids demonstrably play is squarely in the risk zone. He also has Sign in with Apple and a shared leaderboard, which means he is already handling identifiers and display names from possible child users. That is worth a separate look independent of the ads question.

### 5.6 AdMob's own child-directed rules, and what they cost

Google requires calling `setTagForChildDirectedTreatment()` and `setMaxAdContentRating(MAX_AD_CONTENT_RATING_G)` for child-directed or mixed-audience apps, and requires GMA SDK iOS 7.67.0 or later so the advertising ID is not transmitted ([Google AdMob help, "Child-directed setting"](https://support.google.com/admob/answer/6223431)).

Google states the revenue consequence plainly:

> "Ads that are deemed child- and family-friendly are a subset of the broader ads pool. If your app was not previously targeting only child age groups, you might notice fewer filled impressions."

Only Google Play Families self-certified ad networks can serve into child-directed apps, and AdMob automatically blocks everything else in mediation.

**Net effect: the only ads Blake is legally and contractually allowed to run are contextual, non-targeted, G-rated ads from a restricted pool with reduced fill. That is the lowest-paying ad inventory that exists.** The $2 eCPM in my model above is the right end of the range for him, not the $5.

### 5.7 Verdict on ads

Do not do it. Not "probably not". The revenue is $2 to $4 a month at his current scale, the compliance surface is a US federal privacy statute plus two Apple guidelines plus a Google policy, and the product cost is putting pop-ups in a game his family plays. There is no version of this trade that makes sense.

## PART 6: Apple's cut, and everything you must set up first

### 6.1 Commission

- **Standard rate: 30%** of the price paid by the customer, on paid app downloads and in-app purchases.
- **App Store Small Business Program: 15%.** Blake qualifies. From [Apple's page](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/): "It features a reduced commission rate of 15% on paid apps and In-App Purchases." Eligibility: "Existing developers who made up to 1 million USD in proceeds in the prior calendar year for all their apps, as well as **developers new to the App Store**, can qualify."
- **No commission at all on advertising revenue.** Ads are not an IAP.

**How to enrol** (verbatim from Apple): be the Account Holder in the Apple Developer Program; "Review and accept the latest Paid Apps agreement (Schedule 2 to the Apple Developer Program License Agreement) in App Store Connect"; and list any Associated Developer Accounts. Timing: "Your proceeds will be adjusted fifteen (15) days after the end of the fiscal calendar month in which your enrollment is approved." So enrol before launch, not after.

If he ever crosses $1M in a calendar year the standard 30% applies to future sales, and he can re-qualify the following year if proceeds drop back below.

### 6.2 Prerequisites before he can charge a single cent

This is a hard gate and it trips people up. In order:

1. **Sign the Paid Apps Agreement** (Schedule 2) in App Store Connect, under Business / Agreements, Tax and Banking. Until this is signed, "you can only offer your app for free" ([App Store Connect Help: Set a price](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-pricing/set-a-price/)).
2. **Submit tax forms.** "All developers must complete a US tax form to comply with the Paid Apps Agreement." A US-based developer completes a **W-9** ([App Store Connect Help: Provide tax information](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-tax-information/provide-tax-information/)). Blake should decide whether this is under his own name/SSN or an LLC/EIN before filling it in, because changing it later is annoying.
3. **Enter banking information.** Cannot be done until tax forms are submitted. ([App Store Connect Help: Enter banking information](https://www.developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-banking-information/enter-banking-information/))
4. Contract typically activates within about 24 hours after all three are done.

**Note:** all of the above is required for a **free app with an in-app purchase**, not just a paid app. So the "free + $4.99 Supporter IAP" recommendation still requires the whole tax and banking setup. There is no way around it.

### 6.3 Getting paid

- Payment is made "within 45 days of the last day of the fiscal month in which the transaction was completed" ([App Store Connect Help: Overview of receiving payments](https://www.developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/getting-paid/overview-of-receiving-payments)).
- **Minimum payment threshold for a US bank account in USD is $0.02**, per [Apple's threshold reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/reporting/minimum-payment-threshold/). The $40 default applies only to currencies not in Apple's table. So unlike AdMob's $100, Apple will pay Blake essentially any amount. This is a real, concrete advantage of IAP over ads at tiny scale.
- Apple collects and remits sales tax / VAT in most jurisdictions, so proceeds arrive net of tax as well as net of commission.

### 6.4 Price changes and the TestFlight transition

- **He can change the price at any time, in either direction.** App Store Connect supports scheduled price changes with definite start and end dates, or permanent changes, and "effective dates can be scheduled from today up to the end of the next calendar year" ([Schedule price changes for apps](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-pricing/schedule-price-changes)).
- **Anyone who acquires the app while it is free owns it forever.** Per Apple developer forum guidance, "a user who 'purchases' the app, even for free, is always entitled to updates of the app even if the price changes." Free-then-paid is a one-way door for the early cohort.
- **TestFlight users do not automatically get the App Store version.** TestFlight is free to testers regardless of the eventual App Store price ([Apple TestFlight](https://developer.apple.com/testflight/)), and builds expire after 90 days. When NASTY! goes live, his family must download the App Store build. If it is paid, they pay. *I could not find an Apple-published statement explicitly confirming that TestFlight testers get no free App Store entitlement, so confirm this with Apple Developer Support before launching paid.* The workaround is **promo codes**: Apple provides 100 free promo codes per app per version, which is more than enough to cover his relatives.
- Practical consequence: TestFlight progress, leaderboard state and Sign in with Apple identity should be checked to make sure they survive the move from the TestFlight bundle to the App Store bundle. Same bundle ID means it should, but it is worth testing deliberately.

## PART 7: Adoption impact of charging

### 7.1 The structural fact

**95.24% of iOS apps are free** as of February 2026 ([Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1020996/distribution-of-free-and-paid-ios-apps/)). A separate count puts it at 2,341,106 free apps against 124,766 paid, about 5.2% paid ([App Store statistics 2026](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/app-store-statistics/)). Paid downloads are a rounding error in the modern App Store.

### 7.2 The conversion data, and how to read it correctly

Reported App Store product-page conversion rates: **freemium apps 1% to 5%, paid apps around 12%** ([AppScreenshotStudio, 2026](https://appscreenshotstudio.com/blog/app-store-conversion-rate-by-business-model-2026)). Games specifically convert at roughly **3% to 5%** ([AppTweak](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/average-app-conversion-rate-per-category)).

**Do not misread the 12% figure.** Paid apps show higher page-to-install conversion because the people who reach a paid app's page have usually already decided to buy it, often arriving from a review or a recommendation. It is a selection effect, not evidence that charging helps. The volume of people reaching the page in the first place is vastly lower. As one analysis puts it: "A free app's install is one tap, a paid app's install is a purchase." Free and freemium models generate far more total downloads because of lower friction, which is why they dominate revenue globally.

*I could not find a credible, controlled study measuring the download drop from switching a specific indie game from free to paid. Anyone quoting a precise multiplier ("paid gets 10x fewer downloads") is estimating. The directional evidence is overwhelming; the exact ratio is not established.*

### 7.3 What this means for an unknown indie with no marketing

For NASTY! specifically, the adoption question is close to moot, and this is worth being blunt about. With no marketing, no press, no ASO investment and no brand, organic App Store discovery for a new board game is approximately zero **whether it is free or paid**. Look at the indie table in Part 2 again: the free-with-no-ads family board game got 3 ratings. Free did not save it.

The people who will download NASTY! are people Blake tells about NASTY!. That audience responds to a personal recommendation, not to a price point. Which means:

- **The revenue difference between free and $2.99 is genuinely small** in absolute dollars, because the denominator is small.
- **The adoption difference is also genuinely small**, because the denominator is small.
- Therefore the decision should turn on the things that are not small: the multiplayer lobby needing people in it, not billing his own family, and keeping the option open if the game ever catches.

That is the reasoning behind the free recommendation. It is not that free will make more money. It is that at this scale neither option makes money, so pick the one with the better downside.

## PART 8: The concrete plan

1. **Enrol in the App Store Small Business Program now**, before launch, so the 15% rate is live from the first sale. Remember the 15-days-after-fiscal-month-end activation lag.
2. **Complete Paid Apps Agreement, W-9, and banking** in App Store Connect. Required even for a free app with one IAP. Decide personal vs LLC before filing the W-9.
3. **Confirm the updated age rating questionnaire is filed** for NASTY! (the Capabilities and in-app-controls sections added in the July 2025 overhaul, deadline was 31 January 2026). Answer "no in-app advertising".
4. **Ship free.** No ads. No ad SDK. No third-party analytics if avoidable.
5. **Add one non-consumable IAP at $4.99**, named something like "Nasty Supporter" or "Family Pack". It should unlock cosmetics only: extra board themes, custom marble or token colours, a supporter marker next to the name on the shared leaderboard. **Nothing that affects gameplay, and nothing that gates multiplayer.** Blake nets $4.24 per purchase at 15%.
   - Expect 1% to 3% conversion. At 1,000 downloads that is 10 to 30 purchases, $42 to $127. Enough to dent the $99, not enough to matter otherwise.
   - It also gives relatives a way to say thank you, which is worth something non-financial.
6. **Write a privacy policy** covering Sign in with Apple, the leaderboard, and the multiplayer server, and be deliberate about what the Deno Deploy server stores about child users. This is needed regardless of monetisation and is the genuinely under-attended risk in this project. COPPA's amended rule has been in full force since 22 April 2026.
7. **Set a review trigger:** if NASTY! passes roughly 10,000 downloads or 2,000 monthly actives, revisit. At that point ad revenue crosses into the hundreds per month and the calculation genuinely changes.
8. **Do not build a subscription.** Board games have no ongoing content to justify recurring billing; it invites churn, refund requests, and App Review scrutiny under guideline 3.1.2.

- **I could not confirm from an Apple-published source what happens to existing TestFlight testers when an app launches paid.** My reading is they must purchase it, with promo codes as the workaround. Confirm with Apple Developer Support.
- **Tenjin's 2026 benchmark report renders its eCPM tables as images**, so the specific per-format eCPM numbers could not be extracted from that source. I used it only for the platform and network share figures, which are in text.
- **Ludo King's 19,000 US ratings badly understates its global scale** (it is one of the largest board games in the world, driven by India). US rating counts are the right comparison for Blake but the wrong measure of the game's absolute size.
- **All App Store prices and rating counts were read on 26 July 2026** and change frequently. Carcassonne in particular was found at $1.49, discounted from a $4.99 list price, so that one is volatile.
- **Nothing here is legal advice.** The COPPA exposure question, especially given Sign in with Apple plus a shared leaderboard in a game children play, is worth twenty minutes with an actual lawyer before public launch, independent of the monetisation decision.
